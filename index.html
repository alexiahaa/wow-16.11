Concerned about someone?

Start with care.
Not a conclusion.

Changes in someone’s behavior can be worrying. Here’s how to notice possible signs, respond safely, and have a conversation without jumping to assumptions.

Explore possible signs See when it’s an emergency  ↓

One sign alone can’t tell you.

There is no reliable way to tell from appearance alone whether someone has used drugs. Effects differ by substance, dose, person, and whether other substances were taken.

Look for a pattern or a sudden change from what’s normal for them. Similar changes can also come from illness, medication, stress, exhaustion, or a mental health crisis. Treat signs as a reason to check in—not proof or a diagnosis.

What you might notice

These are possible clues, not a checklist. A person may show some, all, or none of them.

01 / Behavior

A sudden shift in mood or focus

Unusual excitement, agitation, anxiety, confusion, poor concentration, or becoming unusually withdrawn.

02 / Alertness

Much more or less awake

Restlessness and little sleep, or seeming very drowsy, hard to wake, or less responsive than usual.

03 / Body

Physical changes

Unsteady movement, slurred speech, tremors, sweating, nausea, unusual temperature, or noticeably changed pupils.

04 / Routine

Changes that keep adding up

Disrupted sleep, appetite, responsibilities, relationships, or interests—especially when several changes persist together.

05 / Safety

Riskier decisions than usual

Impaired judgment, unexplained injuries, or taking risks that feel out of character may be reasons to ask how they’re doing.

06 / Context

What’s happening around them

A concerning change after a party, new medication, or a difficult period can add context—but it still doesn’t confirm drug use.

Keep perspective Pupil size, smell, secrecy, or one difficult day cannot identify a substance or prove someone has used it.

Act now if they may be in danger

Some symptoms need emergency help.

Don’t wait to identify a substance or see if things improve. Call your local emergency number if someone:

Can’t be woken or won’t stay awake Is unconscious, collapses, or becomes less responsive.
Has trouble breathing Is breathing slowly, irregularly, gasping, or has blue/grey lips.
Has a seizure or severe confusion Or is dangerously agitated, overheating, or in severe pain.
May have taken too much or mixed substances Even if they are still awake or say they feel fine.
While help is on the way: Stay with them if it’s safe, follow the dispatcher’s instructions, and share what you know about what they may have taken and when. If they’re unconscious but breathing, place them on their side if you can do so safely. Don’t make them vomit, give food or drink, or leave them alone.

A better way to check in

Pick a calm, private moment when they’re not acutely intoxicated. Lead with what you’ve noticed and your concern.

1

Say what you’ve observed

Try: “I’ve noticed you haven’t been sleeping, and you seem unlike yourself. I care about you—how are things going?”

2

Listen before offering solutions

Give them space to talk. Ask open questions, stay curious, and avoid arguing over what you think is happening.

3

Offer one manageable next step

You could help them contact a doctor, counselor, or local substance-use service—or go with them if they’d like.

4

Set clear, caring boundaries

You can support someone without accepting unsafe behavior. Protect your own wellbeing and ask someone you trust for help.

What not to do

A calm response is safer and more likely to keep communication open.

  • Don’t accuse or shame. It can shut down a conversation.
  • Don’t guess the substance. Symptoms overlap and can mislead.
  • Don’t let them drive. Arrange a safe ride if they may be impaired.
  • Don’t handle a crisis alone. Get professional help when safety is at risk.

You don’t have to figure it out alone

Support can start with one conversation.

A healthcare professional or local substance-use support service can help you think through what you’re seeing and what to do next. You can reach out for advice even if the person isn’t ready.

If there’s immediate danger
Contact your local emergency services. In the U.S. and Canada, call 911; in the UK and many European countries, call 112 or 999.

If you’re unsure what they took
Contact your local poison center or urgent medical advice line. Don’t wait for symptoms to get worse.
